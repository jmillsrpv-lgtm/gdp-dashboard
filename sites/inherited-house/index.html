    1. What to do in the first 30 days

    Before any big decisions, focus on protecting the property. A vacant house can lose value quickly to break-ins, leaks, and neglect.

    • Secure the house. Lock all doors and windows, and consider changing the locks — you may not know who has keys.
    • Call the insurance company. Tell them the owner has passed and the home may be vacant. Many standard policies limit coverage on vacant homes, so ask about a vacancy policy.
    • Keep the utilities on. Keep power and water on at a minimum; set the thermostat to protect pipes in winter.
    • Forward the mail. Bills, tax notices and mortgage statements will help the executor understand what's owed.
    • Find the paperwork. Look for a will, the deed, mortgage statements, property tax bills, and insurance policies.
    • Don't sell or give away belongings yet. Until the estate is sorted out, personal property may belong to the estate as a whole.
    • Get several death certificates. You'll need certified copies for banks, insurance, and the court.
    • Keep up with the essentials. Mortgage payments, property taxes and insurance still come due — talk with the other heirs about who will cover them.

    2. Probate in Mississippi, in plain English

    Probate is the court process that confirms who inherits and gives someone legal authority to handle the estate. In Mississippi, estates are handled by the Chancery Court in the county where the person lived — for example, the Hinds, Madison, or Rankin County Chancery Clerk.

    If there was a will

    The person named as executor usually files the will with the Chancery Court and asks to be appointed. Once the court issues Letters Testamentary, the executor can manage — and, if needed, sell — the house according to the will and the court's requirements.

    If there was no will

    Mississippi law decides who the heirs are. A family member typically asks the court to be appointed administrator, and the court may need to formally determine the heirs. This can take longer, especially with several heirs.

    If the house was in a trust or had a survivorship deed

    The house may pass without going through probate at all. The trustee or surviving owner can often act much sooner.

    Good to know: You usually can't complete a sale until someone has legal authority to sign the deed — but you can start learning your options, getting the house valued, and talking with family now. Every estate is different, so it's worth a consultation with a Mississippi estate or probate attorney.

    3. Your four options

    There's no single right answer. The best choice depends on the house, your finances, and how the other heirs feel.

    Keep it or move in

    Best if: someone wants to live there and can cover the costs
    Pros
    • Keeps the family home
    • No selling costs
    Cons
    • Taxes, insurance and upkeep
    • May need to buy out other heirs

    Rent it out

    Best if: the house is in good shape and you want income
    Pros
    • Monthly income
    • Keep the long-term value
    Cons
    • Repairs to get rent-ready
    • Being a landlord (or paying a manager)

    List it with an agent

    Best if: the house is updated and you have time
    Pros
    • Usually the highest sale price
    • Full market exposure
    Cons
    • Clean-out, repairs, showings
    • Commissions and a longer timeline

    Sell as-is for cash

    Best if: the house needs work, is full of belongings, or heirs want a quick, simple split
    Pros
    • No repairs or clean-out
    • Fast, certain closing
    • No commissions
    Cons
    • Price is below full retail
    • Compare offers carefully

    Tip: you don't have to choose blind. Many families get both an agent's opinion of value and an as-is cash offer, then compare what they'd actually take home after repairs, commissions, and months of holding costs.

    4. Common questions

    My siblings and I can't agree on what to do. Now what?

    This is very common. Start by getting real numbers — what the house is worth as-is, what it would cost to fix, and what it costs to hold each month. Clear numbers often settle disagreements. One heir can also buy out the others. If you truly can't agree, a Mississippi attorney can explain your options, including court-ordered partition.

    Do I have to pay taxes when I sell an inherited house?

    Often less than people expect. Inherited property generally gets a "stepped-up" tax basis to its value at the date of death, so selling soon afterward may result in little or no capital gain. Talk to a CPA about your specific situation.

    There's still a mortgage. What happens to it?

    The loan doesn't disappear, but heirs are generally not personally responsible for it. Federal law typically allows a relative who inherits the home to take it over without the loan being called due. If the house is sold, the mortgage is paid off from the proceeds at closing.

    The house is full of stuff. Do we need to clean it out before selling?

    Only if you list it traditionally. Cash buyers commonly purchase houses with belongings still inside — take what matters to you and leave the rest.

    The property taxes are behind. Is that a problem?

    It's something to address soon. Unpaid Mississippi property taxes can lead to a tax sale. Back taxes can usually be paid from the sale proceeds at closing, so a sale is often still possible.

    How long does all this take?

    A simple estate can move in a few months; estates with no will, many heirs, or disputes can take longer. Properties in a trust can often be sold much sooner.
